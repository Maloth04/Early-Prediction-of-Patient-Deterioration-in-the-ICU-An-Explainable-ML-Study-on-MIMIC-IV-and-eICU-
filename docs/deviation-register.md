This appendix lists every substantive divergence between the approved research proposal and the work delivered in this thesis. It exists because the proposal is a pre-specified analysis plan, and a pre-specified plan is only worth something if departures from it are reported rather than absorbed. Several of the entries below are discussed at length in the body; several are recorded here for the first time. The register makes no distinction between the two, because the reader’s interest is in what changed, not in where it happened to be mentioned.

Three general observations precede the table. First, the largest single deviation, the absence of geographic external validation, is not the most instructive one: it follows from a data-access failure and is discussed in Section 5.7 and Appendix A. The more instructive deviations are the small methodological substitutions, because each was made for a local reason that seemed sufficient at the time and none was recorded when it was made. Second, the proposal committed to more than thirty distinct deliverables across a sixteen-week schedule. That was over-committed rather than ambitious, and the pattern of omissions below is largely a schedule artefact rather than a series of independent decisions. Third, the proposal placed a hard go/no-go checkpoint at the end of week five, requiring that the endpoint be shown reliably definable in both databases before any modelling began, with protocol revision to follow if it failed. That check would have failed. It was not executed as a checkpoint, and the re-scoping it was designed to force happened during write-up instead. Of everything in this register, that is the deviation with the clearest lesson.

## D.3 Delivered without pre-specification

The following analyses are not in the proposal. All are exploratory in the formal sense, whatever weight the discussion places on them, and the thesis labels the first as post hoc wherever it appears. They are listed together here so that a reader can see at a glance which parts of the argument rest on registered analyses and which do not: the two-block feature decomposition and everything built on it, including the separation of recency from presence, the per-variable presence rates, the reportable procedure in Section 4.8.6 and the two robustness tests in Section 4.8.7; the reconstruction of NEWS2 as a clinical benchmark; the behaviour-only model as a third decision-curve comparator; the alarm-burden translation into alerts per bed-day; the disagreement-targeted alerting rule; the endpoint-validity work in Section 4.10, including deaths as a competing risk, the care-unit analysis, the corrected-endpoint re-test, and the time-of-day and observation-intensity analyses; the size-matched contemporaneous reference against which the temporal transport test of Section 4.11 is judged; the falsifiable transport prediction in Section 5.8; and the trial execution of the external-validation protocol in Section 4.12 together with everything in it, namely the harmonised 75-predictor set, the two independent executions, the recency-transport comparison of Table 4.42, the sub-block analysis of Table 4.43, the feature-removal measurements in Section 4.12.3 and the attribution-rank comparison between cohorts in Section 4.12.4, including the first application of H3’s registered union statistic to an institutional comparison and the recomputation of both rank statistics on centred attributions; and the precision calculus of Appendix B.4, computed during write-up and reported there because it shows the registered precondition to have been too low for the registered criterion. The headline contribution of this thesis is in that list rather than among the registered hypotheses, which is worth stating plainly: the pre-specified work was executed and is reported, and the results that changed what the thesis argues were not pre-specified.
